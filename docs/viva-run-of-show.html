The run of show

Fifteen minutes, seven segments. The segments are numbered because they are a sequence — the argument only lands if it is made in this order: here is the city → here is what we predict → here is what we simulate → here is what we optimise → here is what it measured.

0:00 The problemno screen · look at them, not the laptop 90s

Do not open with architecture. Open with the decision a real person has to make.

“A city planner in Belagavi has to decide where to put forty docking stations, or whether a bike lane on Congress Road actually reduces congestion. Today that decision gets made on intuition and tested by building it — and if it's wrong, the money is spent and the road is dug up. This project is a virtual copy of the city where you test the policy before you build it.”

Then, one sentence on what makes it more than a dashboard: “Anyone can plot scooters on a map. The point here is the counterfactual — run the same city twice, once with your policy and once without, same random seed, and diff the results.”

1:30 Livethe city is real 2 min
  • Zoom in until street names are legible. Point at one you recognise.
  • Congestion recolours every three seconds over a WebSocket — mention it once, do not wait for it.
  • Click a coloured road: name, congestion percentage.
“These are 9,212 real street segments imported from OpenStreetMap — 832 km, 1,408 of them named. The routing and the simulation both run on this graph, not on a drawing of a city.”
3:30 Datawhere the machine learning is 2 min

This is the segment that answers “where's the AI?” before anyone asks it.

  • Headline tiles: model MAE against the seasonal-naïve baseline, and the skill ratio.
  • Forecast chart with its 80% interval — point at the shading.
  • Model registry: every version, its metrics, and which one is active.
“LightGBM forecasting trips per zone per fifteen minutes. MAE 0.242 against a seasonal-naïve baseline of 0.610 — a skill ratio of 0.397, where the design document asked for 0.70 or better. The split is chronological, not random, and the evaluation harness refuses a split where the validation window starts before the training data ends. A random split on a time series would let the model see Tuesday evening while predicting Tuesday morning.”

If they look interested, add: a model with no recorded evaluation never gets the is_active flag. The registry enforces it.

5:30 Simthe centrepiece — give it the most time 4 min
  • Four scenarios. Baseline is the city as it runs today; the other three each pull exactly one lever, so any difference is attributable to that lever.
  • Select Protected bike lanes. Press Run simulation. It finishes in about 200 ms.
  • Read the results tiles. Then scroll to why demand went unmet.
  • Compare against → Baseline. This is the moment the project justifies its name.
“Protected bike lanes cut the incident rate 31% while slightly increasing trips served. And the seed line — same seed and same scenario produce identical metrics, with a test asserting exactly that. A simulation you cannot reproduce isn't evidence.”
The best single moment in the demo

Every unmet trip in the baseline is “no charged vehicle” — not “not enough scooters”. The simulator found that on its own.

“The answer this gives a planner isn't buy more vehicles. It's build charging infrastructure. That's the kind of finding the module exists to produce, and nobody coded that conclusion in.”

9:30 Fleet → Rebalancingthe optimisation module 2 min 30s
  • The table shows what each zone has against its target.
  • Press Solve rebalancing. Six vehicles, ~20 km, imbalance 7.8 → 1.8.
  • Drag “worth driving N km” down to 1 and solve again — the plan empties, and the solver says why.
“This is a min-cost transportation problem solved with linear programming. The target is each zone's demand-weighted share of the fleet that exists — rebalancing can't create vehicles, it decides where the ones you have should stand, so the gaps sum to zero. And it returns whole vehicles without any rounding, because a transportation problem's constraint matrix is totally unimodular.”

That last clause is worth memorising — it is the most mathematically substantial sentence in the whole demo, and it is true.

12:00 Adminmeasurement and governance 2 min
  • KPI tiles across 30 days: 50,518 trips, 13,388 kg CO₂ avoided.
  • Point at Where vehicles accumulate — Shahapur −1,253, Sadashiv Nagar +936.
  • Emission factors, each with a cited source. Press Export CSV.
“Two modules describing the same phenomenon from opposite ends: this chart shows vehicles draining out of Shahapur overnight, and the rebalancing planner you just saw is what corrects it. Every number here is read from nightly rollup tables — nothing aggregates 73,000 trips at request time.”

On emissions, say the assumption out loud: every trip is credited as replacing a car trip of equal distance. The app prints that beside the number rather than burying it. Examiners reward stated assumptions.

14:00 GBFS, and closesecond browser tab 1 min

Switch to the tab you opened earlier: localhost:4000/gbfs/2.3/free_bike_status.json, no login.

“That's a public GBFS 2.3 feed — the open standard, so any journey planner could consume this fleet without integrating against our private API. And what's deliberately not in it: only available vehicles, because an in-use vehicle's position is a rider's live location. The vehicle IDs rotate hourly so nobody polling the feed can follow one vehicle across days.”

Close on scope, unprompted. It is far stronger coming from you than dragged out of you.

One number to be careful with

If you claim the min-cost solver beats the greedy one it replaced — −18.4% unserved for 78 van-km, versus 13.0% for 204 km — say explicitly that this was measured on a deliberately thin fleet where rebalancing bites hardest. It is not the −4.2% on screen. Both are true; they are different configurations, and conflating them is the kind of thing an examiner catches.

Questions they will ask

Where is the AI in this?

Two places, both load-bearing. LightGBM forecasting demand per zone per fifteen minutes, evaluated against a seasonal-naïve baseline with a chronological split. And A\* search with a multi-objective cost function for routing — the same graph, three different weightings of time, safety and gradient. The rebalancing solver is optimisation rather than learning, and it is worth saying so rather than overselling it.

Why MySQL and not MongoDB?

Trips, fares, vehicles and incidents are transactional records with real constraints — a trip cannot reference a vehicle that does not exist. That is a relational problem. MySQL 8.4 also gives spatial types with SRID 4326 and spatial indexes, which the whole map depends on.

Is any of this real data?

Be direct: the street network is real — genuine OpenStreetMap geometry for Belagavi. The trips and telemetry are synthetic, generated with realistic daily and weekly patterns, because real rider data is personal and we do not have it. Say this before they ask; volunteering it reads as rigour, being caught reads as spin.

Why doesn't the simulation route riders street by street?

A deliberate choice, written up as ADR 0003. Riders travel zone centroid to zone centroid at a congestion-adjusted speed. Routing 30,000 riders through A\* would take minutes instead of 200 ms, and it would not move the metrics a planner acts on — wait time and unmet demand are set by where the vehicles are, not which street was taken. The router is the microscopic view; the simulator is the city-scale one.

How do you know the model isn't just memorising?

The split is chronological and enforced in code — assert_chronological_split raises if the validation window starts at or before the last training timestamp. Rolling means exclude the current value so the target cannot leak into its own feature, and lags are scoped per zone so a global shift cannot pull another zone's value in.

Why is the safety incident count a decimal?

Because it is an expected value, not a count. At realistic injury rates a 24-hour run samples zero or one incident, so a sampled figure would make the bike-lane lever look better or worse depending on the random seed. Summing per-trip risk gives the unbiased estimate with none of the variance — which is why a 31% reduction reads as 31% every time.

Why three services instead of one?

Express owns identity, transactions and the public contract, and is the only writer to MySQL. FastAPI owns everything numeric and holds no database credentials at all — every request carries the data it needs, which is what keeps it stateless. Workers run anything longer than a request. The browser never talks to the Python service.

Why isn't it finished?

Do not apologise. “Eleven of sixteen modules. The ones built are the ones that carry the argument — forecast, simulate, optimise, measure. The five I left are mostly CRUD over a schema that already exists: parking, notifications, billing, emergency response. Each one has a note in the module document saying it was skipped and why, so nothing looks forgotten.”

What would you do next?

Have one specific answer ready rather than a list. The strongest: “Charging infrastructure planning. The simulator already told us every unmet trip is a flat battery, not a missing vehicle — so the next module is deciding where to put charging points, and the simulator is how I'd evaluate it.” That shows the system produced a finding that drives the roadmap.

If the whole demo will not start

Run npm run demo:check on screen. It prints eight checks and the exact command to fix any failure. Doing that in front of an examiner is not an admission of failure — it demonstrates the project has operational tooling, which most student projects do not.
